# *The Devil's Star* (2003) — Jo Nesbø
### Harry Hole Series, Book 5 (Norwegian: *Marekors*, literally "The Nightmare Cross")

---

## Premise


*The Devil's Star* (Norwegian: *Marekors*, 2003) is a crime novel by Jo Nesbø, the fifth in the Harry Hole series. The story moves between two parallel themes — the appearance of a new serial killer terrorizing Oslo, and Harry Hole's ongoing feud with the corrupt and utterly ruthless fellow police officer Tom Waaler, which was already a major part of the plot of the two previous books, *The Redbreast* and *Nemesis*.
 
Eventually, the two issues converge — enabling Harry to resolve both in the course of a single cataclysmic night.

## True Final Resolution / Ending

The novel resolves two entirely separate criminal plots in a single night.

**The serial killer plot:** 
Wilhelm discovered Lisbeth's affair with Sven during their honeymoon and orchestrated the serial killings as revenge, framing Sven while murdering Lisbeth. Forensic analysis of fennel seeds found under Lisbeth's fingernail confirms that Wilhelm was in physical contact with Lisbeth after he reported her missing. Harry confronts Wilhelm in his flat, having climbed across the rooftops in a rainstorm. Wilhelm freely confesses: He found love letters revealing the affair, traveled to Prague to identify Sven, then designed the pentagram on a map of Oslo using his own address and Olaug's house as two fixed points, constructing the other murder locations geometrically. He killed Marius Veland first, then Camilla Loen and Barbara Svendsen as "scenery" to simulate a serial killer, and finally shot Lisbeth, sealing her body inside the waterbed.
 
Wilhelm slips outside and hangs himself in the yard.


**The Waaler plot:** 
Harry then discovers a voicemail from Waaler, who is standing over sleeping Oleg in Rakel's house, demanding Harry bring Sven to the student building or the boy dies. Harry races there, hiding a gun inside the lift door handle.
 
In the ensuing confrontation, Harry stabs Waaler in the collarbone with a chisel, paralyzing his gun arm. When Waaler retrieves the gun and grabs Oleg through the lift's iron grille, Harry snaps a handcuff onto Waaler's wrist and presses the basement button. The descending lift traps Waaler's arm between the grille and the door frame, killing him.


**Aftermath:** 
In the aftermath, Sven is placed in protective custody. The head of Kripos apologizes to Harry and offers his job back while probing how Harry will present the case, hinting at concern over exposure of corruption within the force. Harry gives a guarded answer. He tells Rakel he has quit the police and accepted a job as a taxi driver. She asks him to come by in the morning with no plans, and he agrees. As dawn approaches, Harry sits alone in his flat, tasting plaster dust on his fingers from the chisel he carried from Wilhelm's flat, where the devil's star was carved into the wall.

## The Deceptive Red Herrings / False Leads

**Red Herring 1 — Sven Sivertsen as the serial killer.** 
The five-pointed diamonds found on the victims provide a major clue as to the next possible murder locations, which are kept under surveillance: one is in a student residence hall and the other is a house on the outskirts of Oslo, owned by Olaug Sivertsen. While investigating this house, Lønn discovers that the likely murderer is Olaug's son, Sven.
 Sven is arrested as the prime suspect — he is a diamond smuggler who visits Oslo every five days, his mother's house is one of the pentagram's five points, and he possesses the same red diamonds. He is entirely innocent of the murders; he was unknowingly supplying the actual killer.

**Red Herring 2 — The occult/ritualistic serial killer framing.** 
Nesbø cleverly withholds a key element as he spins *The Devil's Star*'s plot: the murderer's motive. Though the stylistic precision of the killings display clear intent, the dead women show no sign of assault and seem to bear no relation to one another.
 The pentagram, the five-day intervals, the severed fingers, and the hidden diamonds all suggest a ritualistic serial predator with occult motivations. In reality, 
Wilhelm's plan is to conceal a crime of jealousy under the trappings of religious fanaticism.


**Red Herring 3 — Anders Nygård (Vibeke's partner).** The novel's opening chapters establish Vibeke Knutsen and her partner Anders Nygård in a tense, secretive domestic situation, with Anders frequently absent on "business." The narrative structure invites suspicion of Anders as a possible suspect, but he is never implicated in the murders.

**Red Herring 4 — The Waaler plot as the primary threat.** For much of the novel, Harry's obsession with Waaler dominates his attention and the reader's focus, making the serial killer investigation feel secondary. 
*The Devil's Star* is more than a whodunit; while piecing together the serial killer's trail, Harry must grapple with the remains of his relationship with the unforgettable Rakel, decrypt Tom's double-dealings, and protect his own life.
 The convergence of both plots in the final night is the structural surprise.

## Systemic & Thematic Nuance

The novel's deepest critique is not of crime but of institutional self-protection. 
The novel illustrates institutional decay via examples of internal cover-ups, where evidence of wrongdoing is suppressed to protect influential figures, contrasting the organized malfeasance of officials with more visible street-level crimes. Harry's professional isolation stems from his prior whistleblowing against Waaler's operations, positioning him as an outcast within a system that penalizes integrity rather than complicity, thereby amplifying the sense of systemic betrayal.


The institution does not simply fail to catch Waaler — it actively shields him. 
Harry attempts to convince the Chief Inspector that his colleague, Tom Waaler, is a criminal arms dealer known as 'The Prince' and mastermind behind the murder of Ellen Gjelsten. Due to a lack of evidence, Hole is refused and goes on an alcoholic binge.
 The system's response to Harry's correct accusation is to threaten him with dismissal, not to investigate. Waaler's power derives precisely from his institutional respectability: 
as an alcoholic, Hole's evidence would not be sufficient to convict him if he went to his superiors.
 The corrupt officer is protected not by active conspiracy but by the bureaucratic logic that discredits the unreliable witness.

Harry himself is not morally clean. He is offered a place in Waaler's criminal network and does not immediately refuse. 
Waaler privately offers Harry a place in his criminal network, framing his smuggling as a necessary form of justice. Harry does not refuse outright.
 His alcoholism is not merely a personal failing but a direct consequence of institutional betrayal — the system's refusal to act on Ellen's murder drove him to the bottle. His obsession with Waaler has also made him a worse detective and a worse partner to Rakel, meaning his pursuit of justice has itself caused collateral damage to innocent people.

Wilhelm Barli's crimes expose a different moral failure: the possessive logic of romantic love taken to its extreme. His murders are not the work of a monster but of a man whose sense of ownership over his wife — and whose theatrical intelligence — transforms private jealousy into public spectacle. 
While Barli's obsession with creating a fake, satanic narrative with this much detail can sound absurd at first, it is important to remember that he is a thespian with a clear affinity for the theatrical. By constructing a story where he himself is one of the victims, Barli successfully redirects police attention away from vague details or recurring patterns.
 The novel refuses to make him simply a monster: his grief is real, his intelligence is formidable, and his suicide is a final theatrical act.


Nesbø critiques Norwegian bureaucracy and power structures by portraying how rigid hierarchies enable corruption to flourish unchecked, drawing implicit parallels to real-world concerns about accountability in public institutions.

## Structural Metaphors

**The pentagram as geometric logic.** The devil's star is not merely a symbol of evil — it is a geometric tool. 
Harry cracks a numerical code: The number five connects the five-pointed diamond, five fingers, five days between murders, the approximate time of five o'clock, and the fifth-floor locations. Drawing a pentagram, a five-pointed star symbol with deep occult associations, on a map of Oslo, Harry finds the three crime scenes fall on three of its points.
 The killer has used the occult symbol as a rational, spatial planning instrument. This is the novel's central philosophical joke: the sign of the irrational is deployed with perfect rationality. The "devil" in the star is not supernatural but human — jealousy, calculation, and theatrical intelligence.

**Harry's claustrophobia and the lift.** 
They head back to the student block where the first murder happened, both because it's a good place to hide and because Nesbø needs somewhere with a lift for the climax. Harry is afraid of lifts — heroes in thrillers are allotted one phobia per book, provided they promise to confront them at the end.
 The lift is the novel's structural fulcrum: the enclosed, descending space where Harry must confront both his physical fear and his moral reckoning with Waaler. The mechanism of Waaler's death — trapped between the grille and the door frame by a descending lift — literalises the idea of the corrupt system closing in on itself.

**Harry's profession as metaphor.** Harry is a detective who cannot detect the corruption within his own institution. His investigative brilliance is repeatedly neutralised by his personal unreliability (alcoholism) and his institutional powerlessness (his superiors refuse to believe him). The novel argues that the detective's function — to reveal hidden truth — is structurally undermined when the institution that employs him has a vested interest in concealment. 
Harry's professional isolation stems from his prior whistleblowing against Waaler's operations, positioning him as an outcast within a system that penalizes integrity rather than complicity.


**The Oslo summer heat.** 
Nesbø sets this in Oslo during the summer, amid the warp and woof of urban life — students, theatre, business. The mounting heat wave provides an atmospheric backdrop as we await the storm to break.
 The heat is not incidental: it is a pressure-cooker metaphor for the accumulation of suppressed truths (Waaler's crimes, Wilhelm's secret, Harry's alcoholism) that must eventually rupture.

## Key Adaptation Differences

**Netflix series: *Jo Nesbø's Detective Hole* (2026), directed by Øystein Karlsen, script by Jo Nesbø himself.**


The series stars Tobias Santelmann as Harry Hole, Joel Kinnaman as Tom Waaler and Pia Tjelta as Rakel Fauke, and premiered in March 2026.


**Ellen Gjelten's murder:** This is the most significant divergence. 
The way that the character's death plays out in *Detective Hole* comes with major changes from the book: the 2000 book saw her killed by a neo-Nazi with ties to the mysterious arms dealer, The Prince. Moreover, Ellen's death in *The Redbreast* came right on the heels of her discovering The Prince's identity, being killed before she could identify him to Hole. In Netflix's *Detective Hole*, audiences are not only immediately told that Waaler is The Prince, but he is the one who kills her to cover his identity up, and frames it on the dealer he's hiding in his remote cabin.


**Harry's institutional standing:** 
In *The Devil's Star*, Hole is denied permission from his Chief Inspector to pursue an investigation into Waaler being both The Prince and tied to Ellen's murder, leading to an alcohol-fueled downward spiral that also nearly results in his firing. In the show, however, the Chief Inspector approves his investigation, albeit with the caveat that he has to do so covertly.


**Wilhelm Barli's suicide:** In the novel, 
Wilhelm slips outside and hangs himself in the yard.
 In the Netflix series, 
in the final moments, when Detective Hole was inches away from catching Willy, he did one final theatrical act by jumping from the balcony of his house, onto metal pikes arranged in the shape of a pentagram, which pierced his body, eventually killing him.


**Post-Waaler conspiracy twist (series only):** The Netflix series adds a final twist absent from the novel. 
Chief Superintendent Agnes Sjolid appreciated Hole's efforts in the serial killer case and ordered him to continue his investigation into Tom's secretive group. Agnes believed that many of her officers were linked to the secretive group and it was important to eradicate them from the department. In a final twist, the series takes us to one of these masked meetings and it is revealed that one of these high-ranking members is none other than Agnes Sjolid.
 This character and twist do not exist in the source novel.

**The backstory framing:** 
The series follows Detective Harry Hole of the Oslo Police Department, who struggles to piece together a 5-year-old bank robbery that took his colleague Lars's life. Since Harry was driving under the influence of alcohol that fateful night, he blames himself for Lars' death.
 This bank robbery cold case is a significant structural addition to the television adaptation, replacing the multi-novel backstory of the books.
