# And Then There Were None
**Agatha Christie, 1939**

---

## Premise


*And Then There Were None* is a mystery novel by the English writer Agatha Christie, who described it as the most difficult of her books to write.
 
First published in the United Kingdom by the Collins Crime Club in 1939 as *Ten Little Niggers*, after an 1869 minstrel song that serves as a major plot element, the US edition followed in 1940 with the title *And Then There Were None*, taken from the last five words of the song.
 
Ten strangers are invited to an island off the coast of Devon, England, by a person known only as U. N. Owen; the guests are soon accused of being murderers by a disembodied voice, and one by one they are killed in ways that correspond to a nursery rhyme called "Ten Little Indians."

## True Final Resolution / Ending


*And Then There Were None* has an ending that is unusually grim among Christie's novels: the murders are explained but unresolved, the killer eludes capture, and every character meets their demise.


The sequence of deaths, in order, is: Anthony Marston (poisoned whiskey, first evening) → Ethel Rogers (poisoned in sleep) → General Macarthur (bludgeoned at the cliff) → Thomas Rogers (hatchet to the neck while chopping wood) → Emily Brent (injected with poison) → Justice Wargrave (apparently shot — this is the faked death) → Dr. Armstrong (drowned by Wargrave after being lured to the cliff) → William Blore (marble clock-bear pushed from a window) → Philip Lombard (shot by Vera) → Vera Claythorne (hangs herself).


Vera, Lombard, and Blore go outside, resolving to stay in the safety of the open land. Blore decides to go back into the house to get food. The other two hear a crash, and they find someone has pushed a statue out of a second-story window, killing Blore as he approached the house. Vera and Lombard retreat to the shore, where they find Armstrong's drowned body on the beach. Convinced that Lombard is the killer, Vera steals Lombard's gun and shoots him.
 
She returns to her bedroom to rest, happy to have survived. But upon finding a noose waiting for her in her room, she feels a strange compulsion to enact the last line of the nursery rhyme, and hangs herself.



The mystery baffles the police until a manuscript in a bottle is found. The late Judge Wargrave wrote the manuscript explaining that he planned the murders because he wanted to punish those whose crimes are not punishable under law.


In the manuscript, 
posing as one of the guests, he decided to kill them in order of increasing guilt. Once it became clear that the killer was one of the group, Wargrave tricked Dr. Armstrong into helping him fake his own death as part of a fictitious scheme to trap the murderer into incriminating himself. After all the others were dead, Wargrave shot himself, making sure that his true death matched his staged death recorded in the guests' diaries, so that investigators would be left with "ten dead bodies and an unsolved problem."


Wargrave's self-described motive is a paradox: 
Wargrave's confession exposes a complex interplay between his sadistic impulses and a stringent sense of justice. Wargrave recounts that from an early age he felt a compulsion to kill but was equally driven by a belief in justice, targeting only those he deemed deserving of punishment. These paradoxical impulses led him to a career in law, where he could exercise his moral judgments within societal boundaries.


Crucially, 
in a revealing twist, the murderer confesses to killing the victims in reverse order of their perceived guilt, intensifying the psychological torture for those deemed most guilty.
 
The final five are all guilty of betraying a trust: Dr. Armstrong killed a patient by his drunken negligence; Blore, a police officer, gave false testimony against an innocent man; Lombard betrayed men under his command; but Wargrave judged Vera Claythorne the worst of the lot for killing a child in her care.

## The Deceptive Red Herrings / False Leads

The novel's misdirection is structural rather than episodic — Christie does not plant a single false suspect so much as she makes every surviving guest a plausible killer at every moment.

**Wargrave's faked death** is the central deception. He stages his own murder mid-novel — apparently shot in the forehead, dressed in judicial robes, with Armstrong as unwitting accomplice — removing himself from the suspect pool entirely. 
The judge allegedly dies from a gunshot, aligning with the sixth
 verse of the rhyme. For the remainder of the novel, the reader and surviving characters reason among only the remaining guests, never reconsidering Wargrave.

**Dr. Armstrong** is the most heavily suspected character among the survivors. 
Blore accuses Armstrong of possibly causing Mrs. Rogers's death.
 
Vera suspects the doctor, and Lombard suspects Wargrave.
 Armstrong's medical knowledge makes him the obvious candidate for the poisonings, and his nervous, gullible personality reads as guilt. In fact, he is a victim being manipulated.

**Philip Lombard** is the red herring for Vera specifically. His possession of a revolver (brought because he was hired to expect trouble), his admitted past crime, his predatory manner, and his survival to the final two make him the most plausible killer in the novel's closing pages. 
Convinced that Lombard is the killer, Vera steals Lombard's gun and shoots him
 — and she is wrong. He is guilty of his past crime but is not U. N. Owen.

**Wargrave's own courtroom authority** functions as a meta-red herring: his repeated role chairing the group's investigations, his insistence on logical deduction, and his judicial gravitas all serve to position him as the novel's closest thing to a detective-hero, deflecting suspicion entirely.

## Systemic & Thematic Nuance


Christie's novel challenges conventional narratives of crime and punishment by blurring the lines between the innocent and the guilty, the enforcer and the perpetrator. The novel does not follow the typical murder mystery formula; there is no detective hero, no moral high ground, and no clear resolution.


**The failure of the legal system** is the novel's central systemic critique. 
The novel presents two kinds of guilt: personal and legal. The majority of the characters are people who have escaped the latter but are plagued by the former.
 Every guest has committed a killing that the law either could not reach or actively failed to punish: Rogers withheld medicine for inheritance; Blore committed perjury that sent an innocent man to his death; Armstrong operated drunk; Lombard abandoned colonial subjects he considered expendable; Vera manipulated a child's drowning and was cleared by a coroner. The legal system is not a background institution — it is the novel's explicit antagonist, and Wargrave is its monstrous logical extension.

**Wargrave's internal contradiction** refuses any simple reading of him as a villain. 
Wargrave's confession reflects his awareness of his own sadistic tendencies. While the other characters deny their guilt, Wargrave embraces his role as both judge and executioner, reveling in the power to condemn and relishing the act of murder. This self-awareness sets him apart, highlighting the human capacity for both cruelty and rationalization.
 He is not a hypocrite in the way his victims are — he knows exactly what he is — but his "justice" is indistinguishable from sadism.

**The guests' self-justifications** are the novel's moral anatomy. 
Christie explores the concept of justice through her characters, who rationalize their own past actions, even when those actions have led to others' deaths. This self-justification is what brings them under Wargrave's judgmental gaze, as he sees them as guilty individuals who have escaped the legal system's grasp.
 Emily Brent is the starkest case: she does not feel guilt at all, which Wargrave reads as the most dangerous form of moral failure. Macarthur, by contrast, is so consumed by guilt that he welcomes death — and is killed relatively mercifully. The novel maps a spectrum of moral self-awareness, none of which saves anyone.

**Class and colonialism** are embedded in the structure. 
The guests' improper behavior and adherence to class distinctions contribute to their vulnerability.
 Lombard's crime against East African tribesmen is treated by him as categorically different from murder — 
he rationalizes his crime against East African tribesmen through a colonialist perspective, reflecting the novel's underlying critique of the era's colonial attitudes, where the value of life is weighed differently based on race and where the white British characters see themselves as inherently more moral.
 The novel does not endorse this view; it places Lombard among the guilty.

**Vera's guilt and the nursery rhyme** interact to produce her suicide — which is not simply Wargrave's manipulation but also the culmination of her own psychological unraveling. 
It is the synergy of her guilt, her proneness to hysteria, and her obsession with the rhyme that aided Wargrave to set up an atmosphere that effectively rouses Vera to hang herself, thereby fulfilling the last line of the rhyme.
 She is simultaneously a murderer, a victim, and her own executioner.

## Structural Metaphors

**The nursery rhyme as judicial sentence.** The "Ten Little Soldiers/Indians" rhyme is not merely a plot device — it is Wargrave's written verdict, delivered in advance. Each verse prescribes a method of death that corresponds to the nature of the victim's crime and their psychological state. The rhyme transforms the island into a courtroom where the sentence has already been handed down; the "trial" is the novel itself. 
Over the next few days, guests continue to be killed off in ways matching the deaths described in the nursery rhyme that hangs on the wall in each of their rooms.


**The island as sealed conscience.** 
The island itself becomes a character, creating a claustrophobic atmosphere.
 Soldier Island is geographically cut off from the mainland — and from the legal and social systems that allowed these crimes to go unpunished. The storm that prevents rescue is not just a plot mechanism; it is the novel's argument that there is no external authority capable of reaching these people. The only justice available is the one they carry inside themselves — guilt — and the one Wargrave imposes from within.

**Wargrave's profession as the novel's philosophical engine.** A judge is, by definition, the person society authorizes to determine guilt and impose death. 
These paradoxical impulses led him to a career in law, where he could exercise his moral judgments within societal boundaries.
 The novel's deepest argument is that the boundary between legitimate judicial killing and murder is institutional, not moral — and that the institution (the law) has already failed. Wargrave simply removes the institutional wrapper. The horror is not that he is monstrous; it is that his logic is coherent.

**The ten figurines as countdown and mirror.** 
The dining-room table initially featured ten Indian figures, but with each death one of the figures disappears.
 The figurines are the guests' objective correlative — their deaths made visible and countable. They also mirror the nursery rhyme's structure, turning the dining table into a scoreboard of guilt. Each disappearing figure is a reminder that the guests are not investigators but defendants.

**The epilogue's manuscript-in-a-bottle** is the novel's final structural irony: the only "solution" to the mystery is one that arrives after all consequences are complete, delivered by the killer himself, and found by chance. 
There is no detective hero, no moral high ground, and no clear resolution.
 The confession does not restore order; it merely explains the disorder. Justice, in Christie's darkest novel, is indistinguishable from its absence.

## Key Adaptation Differences

**Stage play (1943), written by Christie herself:**

Christie's 1943 stage adaptation reworked the ending for Lombard and Vera to be innocent of the crimes of which they were accused, survive, and fall in love with each other. She and the producers agreed that audiences might not flock to a tale with such a grim ending as the novel, nor would it work well dramatically as there would be no one left to tell the story.
 
Some of the names were also changed, e.g., General MacArthur became General McKenzie in both the New York and London productions.
 This "happy ending" — in which the killer is unmasked and stopped, and two survivors escape — became the template for most subsequent adaptations for decades.

**Film: *And Then There Were None* (1945), directed by René Clair:**

A 1945 film adaptation directed by René Clair.
 It follows the stage play's happy ending rather than the novel's. 
Vera returns to the house and finds a noose hanging in the parlor and discovers that Quinncannon — the film's renamed judge character — who is very much alive, is the mysterious Mr. Owen.
 Vera and Lombard (here named "Charles Morley") survive. 
The filmmakers "conceived the picture more or less as a comedy,"
 a tone entirely absent from the novel. The killer's identity is the same (the judge), but he is caught alive rather than dying by his own hand.

**BBC miniseries (2015), written by Sarah Phelps:**

The BBC adaptation was released in the UK on BBC One in three parts, starting on 26th December 2015, written by Sarah Phelps and produced by Mammoth Screen with Agatha Christie Productions.
 
The original ending of Christie's novel is, to all intents and purposes, restored
 — making it the first major English-language screen adaptation to do so. 
Other characters are given different and darker motivations to explain their crimes, particularly Emily Brent, Justice Wargrave and Mr. Blore. More importantly, there is a much more severe societal breakdown as the deadly weekend progresses.
 
Most of the characters in the 2015 series are even more unsympathetic than their book counterparts.


**Soviet film: *Desyat' Negrityat* (1987), directed by Stanislav Govorukhin:**

A Russian adaptation produced and directed by Stanislav Govorukhin. The film concludes with the grim finale from Agatha Christie's original novel, rather than the upbeat ending from the 1943 stage version that most other adaptations chose to follow.
 This makes it, alongside the 2015 BBC series, one of the very few screen adaptations faithful to the novel's bleak conclusion.
