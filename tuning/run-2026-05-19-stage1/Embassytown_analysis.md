# *Embassytown* — China Miéville (2011)
## Definitive Character & Structural Breakdown

---

### Premise


*Embassytown* is a science fiction novel by British author China Miéville whose plot involves the town of Embassytown, the native alien residents known as Ariekei, their Language, and humans' interaction with them.
 
It takes place mostly in the city of the title, on the planet Arieka; Embassytown is a colony of a state called Bremen, and its trade goods — precious metal and alien-influenced biotech — along with its unique position at the edge of the known universe, make it a notable colony.
 
The novel was well reviewed and won the 2012 Locus Award for Best Science Fiction Novel.

### Cast (verified via web search)

**Avice Benner Cho** — protagonist, narrator, Immerser, and living simile — unaffiliated (native Embassytowner, formerly of the Out) — protagonist — alive at close — sources: Wikipedia (Embassytown), Alchetron, SuperSummary


Avice Benner Cho is a human colonist who has returned to Embassytown after years of deep-space adventure; she cannot speak the Ariekei tongue, but she is an indelible part of it, having long ago been made a figure of speech, a living simile in their language.
 
She becomes "the girl who was hurt in the dark and ate what was given to her," representing pain and submission to the Ariekei — a living breathing figure of speech.
 
Miéville tells the story as a first-person narrative through Avice, who prides herself on being a "floaker" — someone practicing "the life-technique of aggregated skill, luck, laziness and chutzpah."

**Scile** — non-native linguist, Avice's husband — unaffiliated (later covert saboteur) — major — alive at close (spared execution) — sources: Wikipedia, Alchetron, TV Tropes


Scile is a non-native linguist and the husband of Avice who believes that Language should remain as it is regardless of the consequences.
 
He wants to prevent further contamination of the Ariekei by outsiders, believing there is something beautiful about their perfectly literal Language, incapable of telling a lie; he comes to assign a kind of divinity to the Hosts, the last honest beings in creation — which leads him to engineer a conspiracy to murder one Ariekei for being exceptionally good at lying, and later to murder Cal in the hopes of ensuring the conflict continues.
 
Scile emerges from hiding and kills Cal by shooting him, just as Surl Tesh-echer was assassinated, but he is spared execution.

Ez/Ra is a new, Bremen-engineered Ambassador consisting of two non-identical people, who becomes known as the "God Drug."
 
It is revealed in a complicated and chilling plot twist that EzRa is not a doubled clone, but a pair of different men connected by implants.
 
EzRa's strange speech causes the Ariekei to get addicted to it, so that every sound they make causes the Hosts to go into shuddering, ecstatic highs.
 
Ez murders Ra, and the Hosts quickly begin to go into withdrawal.

**The Ariekei (the Hosts)** — indigenous alien species of Arieka — Ariekei — collective — fate: transformed, post-Language — sources: Wikipedia, Alchetron, SuperSummary


The Ariekei have two speaking orifices and utter their language through both simultaneously; for them, language, thought and reality are inseparable, hence they cannot understand the speech of individual humans, tell lies or speculate.
 
Few people can speak the language of the Hosts (referred to only as "Language"), as it requires the orator to speak two words at once; those humans who can are genetically-engineered twins known as Ambassadors, bred solely for this purpose — the Ambassadors speak with two mouths and one mind and as such can be understood by the Ariekei, allowing for trade in their valuable biotechnology.

### True Final Resolution / Ending

The crisis is triggered when Ez murders Ra, collapsing the EzRa pairing and sending the addicted Ariekei into catastrophic withdrawal. 
Ez murders Ra, and the Hosts quickly begin to go into withdrawal; they begin invading more and more of Embassytown out of desperation, and the Terre inhabitants are forced to kill those who will not turn away.
 Wyatt reveals that Ez is an empathic anomaly and that anyone with the right neural implants can serve as his counterpart; Cal volunteers, forming the new pairing EzCal, which temporarily sustains the addicted Hosts while Avice works on a permanent cure.


Avice is drawn into a search for a solution, having a special relationship with the Ariekei as a human simile; with assistance from sympathetic Ambassadors, she trains a small group of Hosts to be able to use metaphors and eventually utter lies — due to the interconnectedness of thought and Language, this has the effect of altering their minds, and now the words of Ez/Ra lose their addictive properties.



Scile emerges from hiding and kills Cal by shooting him, just as Surl Tesh-echer was assassinated, but he is spared execution.
 This act of sabotage is ultimately futile: the knowledge of how to create a new Ez-counterpart has already spread. 
The two Ariekei factions reconcile; freed from the literal nature of Language, they are able to learn new forms of communication, which also allows them to speak to non-Ambassador humans.
 
The book ends with the expulsion of the terrorist humans; the Ariekei integrate even further into the remaining human presence, able to communicate with them better than before.
 
Bremen decides to grant the colony some autonomy, instead of annexing it outright as they had planned, simply because going to war with them would be way too much expense for little reward.
 
Avice sets her sights on the Immer again, with the intention of passing beyond Arieka, and thus beyond known Immer space.


The ending is explicitly bittersweet: 
this disaster turns out to be survivable, maybe even ultimately a good thing — Ariekene society is irretrievably altered, the Ariekei learn to speak lies and will never be the same, but this opens their minds to new possibilities, and lays those possibilities before humans as well. The novel ends on a note not merely of hope, but of staunch belief in the bright future humans and Ariekei are going to build together, now that they've left the Garden of Eden.

### The Deceptive Red Herrings / False Leads

*Embassytown* is not a mystery novel in the whodunit sense, but it deploys several sustained misdirections:

**1. The colonial-catastrophe genre expectation.** 
The setup is too blatant, too calculated to recall Empire and its excesses; the references to the Fall and the temptation of knowledge overlaid on this setup cement the reader's conviction that the humans are going to damage the Ariekei horribly — that awareness of the inevitability of a catastrophic fuckup permeates Embassytown.
 The novel then refuses the expected tragic ending, delivering instead a transformed but hopeful resolution.

**2. EzRa as a unified entity.** For the first portion of the novel, EzRa is presented as a functioning (if strange) Ambassador. 
New Ambassador EzRa, despite their job description requiring them to be identical, are in fact different as night and day: one is short and stocky, the other tall and thin, one outgoing, the other introverted, and one selfish and weak-willed, the other much more dedicated — albeit to undermining Embassytown on behalf of Bremen.
 The reader is led to believe the problem is merely political (Wyatt's power play) before the linguistic-addiction catastrophe fully reveals itself.

**3. Scile as a passive idealist.** Scile is introduced as a linguist with a romantic attachment to the purity of Language. 
He wants to prevent further contamination of the Ariekei by outsiders, believing there is something beautiful about their perfectly literal Language, incapable of telling a lie, and comes to assign a kind of divinity to the Hosts.
 His escalation into active sabotage — engineering the murder of Surl/Tesh-echer and ultimately shooting Cal — is the novel's most significant character-level reversal.

**4. The Absurd as the primary threat.** The self-deafened Ariekei faction appears for much of the crisis as the most immediate existential danger to Embassytown. 
It becomes clear that the Absurd hope to wipe out all addicted Hosts and Terre so that the new generation of Hosts can grow up free of the addiction; they gather an army to bring to Embassytown.
 They are ultimately revealed as potential allies once Avice demonstrates that liberation from addiction does not require self-mutilation.

### Systemic & Thematic Nuance

**The Ambassador system as structural violence.** 
Embassytown clones its own pairs of people that achieve higher levels of speech recognition; the pairs of speakers, with names like CalVin or DalTon to indicate how inseparable they are, become Ambassadors, the only ones who can speak to the Ariekei.
 These individuals are bred and conditioned from birth to be a single mind in two bodies — a form of identity erasure that the novel treats as normalised. 
The ingrained taboos against referring to paired Ambassadors as individuals rather than a single person, or trying to tell them apart, extend to cutesy portmanteau names like CalVin or MagDa, and the deference that ordinary Embassytowners feel towards them.
 The Ambassador system is simultaneously the colony's only means of survival and a system that denies its practitioners individual personhood.

**The drug-trade parallel and colonial complicity.** 
In a way, Embassytown works as a critique of colonialism; addiction is an interesting metaphor for the kind of internalized oppression that draws colonized peoples into the cultural and economic orbit of more powerful countries (or planets).
 
In an attempt to survive, the Ambassadors (and Avice) resort to what basically amounts to drug pushing in order to maintain trade.
 The humans are not innocent victims of the crisis they helped create; they actively perpetuate the addiction because their own economic survival depends on it, mirroring the logic of the historical Opium Wars. 
There are explicit parallels with the socio-political tension that arose from the Western powers' determination to maintain a hugely profitable opium trade into China, making addicts of so many of the population; two opium wars and one Boxer rebellion highlighted the physical isolation of the Legations, waiting desperately for relief from their powerful but distant home countries — Embassytown, like the Legations, is dangerously cut off when the crisis hits.


**Scile's conservationism as a form of domination.** Scile's desire to preserve Language in its "pure" state is presented as a kind of love, but it is structurally indistinguishable from the coloniser's desire to keep the colonised in a state of picturesque stasis. His willingness to murder — first Surl/Tesh-echer through conspiracy, then Cal directly — reveals that his reverence for the Ariekei is possessive rather than respectful. 
Miéville has, somewhat manipulatively, constructed his premise so that the eradication of Language, of the Ariekei's unique cultural heritage, by alien interlopers is ultimately held up as a good thing
 — a genuine moral ambiguity the novel does not fully resolve.

**The biological infrastructure as collective body.** 
The collapse of Ariekene society goes right down to their infrastructure — the architecture and technology are all biological, and they become addicted along with the Ariekei.
 The living city's decay is not merely metaphorical: the Ariekei's cognitive crisis is literally embodied in the rotting walls and failing machinery of their world, making the linguistic catastrophe inseparable from physical collapse.

**Ehrsul and the question of personhood.** 
Ehrsul is almost more important in her absence than in her presence; her very nature poses troubling issues of what it is to be human or sentient and whether the difference matters.
 The novel refuses to resolve whether Ehrsul is a person, mirroring its refusal to resolve whether the Ariekei's transformation is liberation or cultural genocide.

### Structural Metaphors

**Avice's trajectory: from simile to metaphor.** The novel's deepest structural argument is encoded in Avice's own linguistic status. She begins as a simile — a literal, enacted comparison, a thing that *is* what it represents, with no gap between signifier and signified. 
She gives the Ariekei knowledge of the metaphor, the vehicle through which one can make an analogy to escape objective truth, and eventually teaches them to tell lies.
 In doing so, she herself becomes a metaphor — a figure whose meaning exceeds her literal existence. The novel argues that the capacity for metaphor, for saying one thing and meaning another, is the precondition for freedom, imagination, and genuine communication.

**The Immer as deep grammar.** 
Avice describes the immer as "langue of which our actuality is a parole" — language of which our actuality is a word — and the universe is interpreted as being "a kind of deep-structured grammar," a foreshadowing of the novel's themes.
 The hyperspace through which Avice travels as an Immerser is itself a structural metaphor: the permanent, unchanging substrate (the immer) underlies the contingent, mutable surface of reality (the manchmal), just as Language underlies and constitutes Ariekei consciousness. Avice's profession as a navigator of this deep grammar positions her as uniquely suited to alter it.

**The dual-timeline structure (Formerly / Latterday).** 
Miéville intersperses the slow-but-steady acceleration of the plot with more worldbuilding-dense flashbacks, using an alternating Formerly/Latterday structure.
 This formal choice enacts the novel's argument about language and time: the past is not dead but is continuously spoken into the present, just as Ariekei similes require the original event to have actually occurred. Avice's narration is itself a retrospective act of storying — she is, as the novel acknowledges, constructing a narrative after the fact, choosing what to reveal and when.

**The colonial outpost as linguistic prison.** 
Embassytown takes place mostly in the city of the title, on the planet Arieka; it is a colony of a state called Bremen, and its unique position at the edge of the known universe makes it a notable colony.
 The physical setting — a human bubble of breathable air surrounded by an alien atmosphere that is toxic to humans — is a spatial metaphor for the novel's central problem: two forms of consciousness that cannot directly inhabit each other's world, mediated only by a small, fragile, and politically compromised class of translators.
