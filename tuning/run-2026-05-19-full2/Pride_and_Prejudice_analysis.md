Here is the complete, spoiler-inclusive analysis of *Pride and Prejudice* (Jane Austen, 1813).

---

**Premise**


*Pride and Prejudice* is a romantic novel of manners by Jane Austen, published anonymously in three volumes in 1813. A classic of English literature written with incisive wit and superb character delineation, it centers on the burgeoning relationship between Elizabeth Bennet and Fitzwilliam Darcy.
 
A novel of manners, it follows the character development of Elizabeth Bennet, the protagonist, who learns about the repercussions of hasty judgments and comes to appreciate the difference between superficial goodness and actual goodness.

**True Final Resolution / Ending**


Elizabeth discovers that Darcy, not Gardiner, paid off Wickham's debts, out of love for her.
 
Bingley and Darcy return to Netherfield, and Bingley finally proposes to an overjoyed Jane.
 
While Darcy goes to London on business, Lady Catherine visits Elizabeth, warning her not to marry Darcy; Elizabeth refuses to promise.
 
Lady Catherine tells Darcy about Elizabeth's impertinence, and her description of Elizabeth's response gives Darcy hope that Elizabeth has had a change of heart. He proposes again, and Elizabeth happily accepts.
 
Elizabeth tells him her prejudice against him had made her blind; Darcy acknowledges that his pride made him act rudely.
 
Both Jane and Elizabeth are married.
 
The story concludes with an "update" a year later, briefly displaying how all of the main characters, many of them in new marriages, are faring.
 Wickham and Lydia remain married but are financially dependent on Darcy and the Bennet family. 
Darcy bribes Wickham to behave honorably to Lydia, but despite the money, it does not seem likely that Wickham will ever change his ways.
 Charlotte Collins remains contentedly settled at Hunsford Parsonage. No character dies in the novel.

**The Deceptive Red Herrings / False Leads**

This is not a mystery novel in the genre sense, but Austen constructs a sustained epistemological misdirection around two characters:

**Wickham as wronged gentleman.** 
Wickham, a handsome young soldier, is friendly toward Elizabeth and tells her how Darcy cruelly cheated him out of an inheritance.
 
With close relations to her own siblings and a keen sense of justice, Elizabeth is predisposed to believe Wickham's story — and it fits perfectly with her own existing prejudices about Darcy.
 This framing dominates the first half of the novel. The truth, revealed in Darcy's letter, is the reverse: 
Wickham was lying about Darcy's so-called "betrayal" — Darcy had offered Wickham the parsonage, but Wickham wanted money instead, which he soon wasted away. Wickham also tried to elope with Georgiana Darcy to gain her inheritance.


**Darcy as irredeemable snob.** Elizabeth's first impression of Darcy — formed at the Meryton ball when he refuses to dance with her and makes a dismissive remark about her appearance — hardens into a settled prejudice. 
Still repelled by his pride and believing Darcy is responsible for Bingley's separation from Jane and for Wickham's misfortune, Elizabeth refuses his first proposal.
 The novel systematically dismantles this reading: Darcy's intervention in the Lydia-Wickham crisis, his warm reception of the Gardiners (tradespeople), and the testimony of his housekeeper Mrs. Reynolds all reveal a man whose outward coldness conceals genuine integrity and generosity.

**Caroline Bingley's claim that Bingley will marry Georgiana Darcy.** 
Bingley suddenly departs for London, and Caroline informs Jane by letter that not only will they not be returning, but moreover her brother is planning to wed Georgiana, Darcy's sister.
 This is a deliberate fabrication by Caroline to separate Jane and Bingley, and it proves false when Bingley returns and proposes to Jane.

**Systemic & Thematic Nuance**

Austen refuses a simple good-versus-evil structure. The novel's moral failures are distributed across every social stratum and every character, including the protagonists.

**Elizabeth's pride in her own perceptiveness is itself a form of prejudice.** 
Elizabeth is keen and perceptive, but her pride in that very ability engenders a prejudice that almost hinders her happy future with Darcy.
 Her intelligence, which the novel celebrates, is also the instrument of her most serious errors of judgment. She is not a reliable reader of character — she is a confident one, which is worse.

**Darcy's class-consciousness is not merely personal vanity but a structural inheritance.** 
Men like Darcy are expected to marry noble, wealthy women in order to maintain the reputation and bloodlines of their houses. As a landowner, Darcy is responsible for the livelihoods of those who live on his property. He is expected to behave as a gentleman, and his wife is expected to contribute wealth and status to Pemberley.
 His first proposal — in which he insults Elizabeth's family while declaring love — is not simply rudeness; it is the collision of genuine feeling with a lifetime of class conditioning.

**Mr. Bennet's detachment is a moral failure, not a charming eccentricity.** 
Though a discerning, well-educated man, Mr. Bennet has made a bad marriage and is resigned to endure it. He is a good-hearted person, but fails his family by remaining sarcastically detached — everything is a joke.
 His failure to supervise Lydia directly enables her elopement with Wickham, nearly destroying all five daughters' prospects. His wit is the novel's most pleasurable voice and simultaneously its most irresponsible one.

**Charlotte Lucas's pragmatic marriage exposes the system's coercion of women.** 
Charlotte marries Mr. Collins for financial security, fearing becoming a burden to her family. Austen uses Charlotte's decision to illustrate how women of the time often married out of convenience rather than love, without condemning her choice.
 Elizabeth's horror at Charlotte's decision is itself a form of privilege: Elizabeth can afford to refuse Collins because her father is still alive. Charlotte cannot.

**The entail as systemic violence.** 
Mr. Bennet, owner of the Longbourn estate, has five daughters, but the estate is entailed and can only be inherited in the male line. Mrs. Bennet and her daughters face living only on the interest from limited sums upon Mr. Bennet's death.
 Mrs. Bennet's obsessive matchmaking — routinely mocked by the novel's ironic narrator — is, at its root, a rational response to a legal structure that renders women economically helpless. 
For the Bennet daughters, marriage must by necessity also function as a business transaction. From a practical perspective, Elizabeth's decision to reject both Mr. Collins and Mr. Darcy is as rash and irresponsible as Mrs. Bennet claims. Mr. Collins stands to inherit Longbourn, meaning that he could have provided Elizabeth, her mother, and sisters with a place to live. By rejecting him, Elizabeth rejects not only her own financial security but also that of Mrs. Bennet and her younger sisters.


**Wickham's villainy is the product of the same class system the novel critiques.** 
Wickham displays a propensity to go to any length to acquire social standing. To this end, he gives Georgiana false promises of marriage, makes false accusations against Darcy to appropriate a share of his inheritance, and elopes with Lydia to avoid the debts he owes to various tradesmen.
 He is not simply a bad individual; he is a man without inherited wealth or land in a society that offers such men no legitimate path to security.

**Structural Metaphors**

**The title itself is the structure.** The novel's two-word title names the two cognitive errors that must be overcome — one by each protagonist — before union is possible. Darcy's pride causes him to look down on Elizabeth's family and to propose in a manner that is simultaneously a declaration of love and an insult. Elizabeth's prejudice causes her to misread Wickham as virtuous and Darcy as corrupt. 
By the end, Austen brings together not just a romance but a moral and emotional journey. The novel shows how easily people misread one another, how pride can isolate, and how prejudice can distort even an intelligent mind.


**The marriage market as epistemological arena.** 
Wealth is a strong theme in the novel, and male characters of marrying age are usually described, first and foremost, by their annual income. Money plays a fundamental role in the marriage market for the young ladies seeking a well-off husband and for men who wish to marry a woman of means.
 The social ritual of courtship — balls, visits, letters, proposals — is the only arena in which women can exercise any agency, and Austen uses it as a laboratory for testing the accuracy of human judgment. Every misreading of character in the novel occurs within a social performance designed to conceal true motives.

**Pemberley as moral revelation.** Elizabeth's visit to Darcy's estate at Pemberley — undertaken when she believes he is absent — functions as the novel's pivotal scene of re-evaluation. The estate's tasteful grandeur, the warmth of the housekeeper Mrs. Reynolds's testimony, and Darcy's unexpectedly gracious behavior toward the Gardiners (tradespeople he would once have disdained) together constitute a form of evidence that no social performance could have provided. The physical space of Pemberley is the argument for Darcy's character that his manner could never make.

**The letter as the novel's true climax.** Darcy's letter to Elizabeth after her rejection of his first proposal is structurally the novel's turning point. It is the moment at which the reader's and Elizabeth's epistemological positions diverge from Wickham's false narrative and begin to converge on the truth. 
Elizabeth realises that she was too proud and misjudged other characters, having the same flaws she once saw in Mr. Darcy.
 The letter — a private, unperformed communication — is more truthful than any spoken exchange in the novel, because it cannot be adjusted in real time to please its audience.

**Key Adaptation Differences**

Two major screen adaptations dominate the cultural reception of the novel: the BBC miniseries (1995, dir. Simon Langton, starring Jennifer Ehle and Colin Firth) and the feature film (2005, dir. Joe Wright, starring Keira Knightley and Matthew Macfadyen).

**Fidelity to the novel.** 
The most accurate adaptation is the 1995 miniseries. Benefited by extra screen time, it does not have to cut minor characters or whole sections of Austen's original dialogue. It is almost an exact page-for-page adaptation of the novel.
 
The 2005 motion picture focuses mainly on Elizabeth and Darcy, taking out many scenes revolving around other characters, including some of Lydia's elopement. The 2005 version runs just over two hours, while the longer BBC 1995 version follows the subplots of the book more carefully.


**The ending controversy (2005 film).** 
The single biggest controversy from *Pride & Prejudice* (2005) was Joe Wright's decision not to end the movie with a wedding. Instead, the film ends with a sentimental scene between the now-married Darcys, enjoying an intimate moment at Pemberley.
 
That decision caused a major backlash from the Jane Austen Society of North America before its release, and the scene was removed from the British release after complaints from preview audiences. The British release instead had a scene where Mr. Bennet blesses Elizabeth and Darcy's union. However, after audiences complained that they were excluded from the true ending, Wright's original conclusion was reinstated.


**Characterization shifts (2005 film).** 
The 2005 film changed the characterization of Mr. and Mrs. Bennet to make them more sympathetic, turning Mr. Bennet into a loving and attentive father and presenting Mrs. Bennet's machinations with understanding instead of scorn. The Bennet family is close-knit in the film; however, Jane Austen presents the family as dysfunctional and unhappy.


**Period setting shift (2005 film).** 
Another large difference between the novel and the 2005 film is the setting. The scriptwriter and director shifted the movie forward by about 20 years, setting it during the 1790s.
