# Tokyo Express
## Seichō Matsumoto, 1958

**By:** Seicho Matsumoto

Two detectives — one in Fukuoka, one in Tokyo — piece together how an apparent love-suicide on a Kyushu beach was in fact a calculated double murder engineered to silence a government corruption linchpin. The crime is less a whodunit than a howdunit: the businessman responsible is visible from the opening pages, but his alibi, built on the precision of Japan's railway timetable, seems impregnable.

## Setting

Post-war Japan, 1957–58. Japan's national railway network — its published timetables, station platforms, and dining-car receipts — functions as both the crime's mechanism and its central metaphor. The title's Japanese original, Ten to Sen ('Points and Lines'), names the geometry of alibi: fixed stations in time and space, connected by routes that can be weaponised by anyone who knows them precisely enough.

## Fukuoka Police

The regional force that first investigates the bodies found on Kashii Beach, inclined to close the case quickly as a love-suicide.

**Torigai Jūtarō** *(protagonist)*

The first investigator on the scene, sharp-minded and unegotistical despite institutional pressure from his boss to close the case quickly. His private moral discomfort — not any systemic mandate — drives him to keep pulling at threads, making him a protagonist operating almost entirely against the grain of the institution he represents.

## Tokyo Metropolitan Police

The Second Investigative Division, focused on white-collar crime, which assigns Mihara to the case after Tokyo connections emerge.

**Kiichi Mihara** *(protagonist)*

The novel's second-half protagonist, younger and even more dedicated than Torigai, whose infectious enthusiasm for the case leads him across the entire rail network of Japan piecing together the timetable alibi. He is the one who ultimately authors the letter to Torigai laying out the full truth — a conclusion that is correct but unprosecutable.

## Yasuda Household

The industrialist and his wife at the centre of the conspiracy — a unit defined outwardly by wealth and status, inwardly by coercion and complicity.

**Tatsuo Yasuda** *(major)*

A successful businessman normalised entirely by the postwar economic order — he did not become corrupt in any dramatic sense but simply cultivated government contacts as standard practice. He engineers the murders not out of personal passion but to protect a bribery network on which his business depends, constructing a timetable-precision alibi in Sapporo while his wife does the actual killing; his guilt is transparent early, yet he escapes prosecution, which is Matsumoto's point about how power protects itself.

**Ryoko Yasuda** *(major)*

Simultaneously perpetrator and victim: a wealthy, educated, beautiful woman whose psychological subjugation to her husband is so complete that she participated in a double murder to preserve their marriage, tolerating his affairs and obeying his demands because his approval is the axis of her identity. The novel refuses to simplify her — she is neither a monster nor merely a tool — and her escape from prosecution is as troubling as her husband's.

## Unnamed Ministry (Government Corruption Network)

The unnamed ministry and its officials whose procurement corruption Sayama facilitated as linchpin; their protection necessitates the murders.

**Kenichi Sayama †** *(major)*

An assistant section chief responsible for procurement and contracts who served as the invisible connective tissue between corrupt ministry officials and industrialists like Yasuda; his death is engineered precisely because, as linchpin, his removal collapses the public prosecutor's entire investigation into the bribery network.

**Unnamed Ministry officials / bribery scandal figures** *(supporting)*

The unnamed ministry officials whose procurement corruption Sayama facilitated; individually unidentified in the novel's text, they form the systemic backdrop that makes the murders necessary and ensures no formal prosecution follows the detectives' discoveries.

## Koyuki Restaurant

The Tokyo restaurant where Toki worked and Yasuda cultivated his government contacts; its staff serve as unwitting instruments in Yasuda's alibi construction.

**Toki Kuwayama †** *(major)*

A waitress at the Koyuki who kept to herself and never flirted with customers, yet was the object of Yasuda's regular attention; her death is a collateral necessity of the cover story — she is killed to make the double suicide staging credible, making her murder the most nakedly utilitarian act in the novel.

**Yaeko and Tomiko** *(supporting)*

Two workers at the Koyuki whom Yasuda manoeuvres into accompanying him to Tokyo Station on October 14th, where they observe Toki and Sayama boarding the Asakaze Express — a manufactured sighting designed to establish the fiction of a lovers' journey to Kyushu and to give the alibi independent human witnesses.
